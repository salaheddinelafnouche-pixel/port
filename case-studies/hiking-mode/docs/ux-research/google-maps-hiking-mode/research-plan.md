# Research plan: Google Maps Hiking Mode

## Decision
Whether Hiking mode should optimize first for **multi-day trail continuity and safety planning** (backpacker-led) while remaining usable for shorter hikes, versus optimizing first for **simple day-hike discovery**.

## Research questions
1. How do long-distance hikers currently combine Google Maps with specialty apps across pre-trip, on-trail, and town days?
2. Which on-trail failures (wrong fork, elevation surprise, dry water, no bailout, offline loss) are most frequent and most stressful?
3. What information must be visible to commit to a next hiking day (distance, elevation, water, camp, bailout, weather)?
4. Where does Google Maps already earn trust, and where does trust break for dirt navigation?
5. How do day and weekend hikers’ jobs differ, and what subset of the backpacker need still applies to them?
6. Would a Hiking mode inside Maps reduce app switching enough to become the default trail companion for a multi-day section?

## Already believed
- Belief: Hikers use Maps for roads and trailheads. Assumption.
- Belief: Specialty apps own trail detail today. Assumption.
- Belief: Offline and bailouts are decision-critical for backpackers. Assumption.
- Belief: Primary design should serve serious backpackers first, stay open to others. Evidence (project brief).

## Must learn
- Actual tool-switching sequence (RQ1)
- Ranked failure modes and emotions on trail (RQ2)
- Minimum viable day-plan inputs (RQ3)
- Trust boundaries for Maps on dirt (RQ4)
- Secondary-user overload vs. value (RQ5)
- Willingness to consolidate into Maps Hiking mode (RQ6)

## Method
- Chosen: **Mixed methods** (per case study brief) — Quantitative survey (n≈120) + Qualitative interviews (n=8) + social listening on Google Maps reviews / outdoor discourse
- Primary instruments: Online survey for prevalence and ranking; 15-question interview guide (3 warm-ups + 12 topic questions with probes) for motivation, abandonment, and language; coded public commentary for triangulation
- Why this method: The decision needs both **how common** failure modes and tool switches are (survey) and **why** hikers trust or abandon Maps on dirt (interviews). Social listening validates that abandonment language appears in the wild.
- Why a single method was not used alone: Survey alone cannot surface planning rituals and trust language; interviews alone cannot size which pains dominate the segment.
- What this method cannot tell us: Live observational trail behavior, true offline failure rates in the field, or production A/B conversion inside Google Maps.

## Participants
- Include: Adults who completed at least one overnight backpacking trip in the last 24 months; subset with multi-day or long-distance section experience; secondary quota of day/weekend hikers who used a phone map on trail
- Exclude: People who only hike guided tours with no personal navigation; people who never use smartphone maps
- How many: Survey ≈120 completers (≈70% backpacker-primary, ≈30% day/weekend); Interviews = 8 (6 backpacker / long-distance, 2 day/weekend)

## Outline

### Survey themes (each maps to RQ + metric)
1. Tool mix frequency in last trip (RQ1) — % using Maps + specialty app same trip
2. Ranked stress events on trail (RQ2) — top-3 selection rate per failure mode
3. Day-commit criteria checklist (RQ3) — % marking each info type “must have”
4. Trust for dirt vs road (RQ4) — Likert trust scores
5. Trip length segment (RQ5) — day / weekend / multi-day / long-distance
6. Intent to try Maps Hiking mode (RQ6) — likelihood if mode existed

### Interview themes (each maps to RQ)
1. Walk me through last multi-day section: tools by stage (RQ1)
2. Tell me about a time navigation or planning failed (RQ2)
3. How you decide tomorrow’s miles (RQ3)
4. Moments you trusted or abandoned Maps (RQ4)
5. What would make you stay in one app on trail (RQ6); lighter probe for day hikers (RQ5)

## Ethics
- Tell participants the purpose and get consent.
- Collect only what answers the research questions.
- Anonymise people in the artifacts.
- This case study uses **synthesized** responses labeled Hypothesis — not live fieldwork.

## How findings update the artifacts
- RQ1–RQ2 confirm or kill empathy-map Pains and journey pain points on trail stages
- RQ3 confirms persona Needs for day planning
- RQ4 updates trust Assumptions in analysis
- RQ5 justifies or trims secondary persona scope
- RQ6 supports or weakens the backpacker-led product decision
